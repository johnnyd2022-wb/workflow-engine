# New Zealand wine producer and seller compliance: research and product opportunity

**Research date:** 8 November 2026  
**Scope:** New Zealand wine businesses from vineyard/grape intake through winemaking, packaging, domestic sale, export and end-of-life/recall. Covers producers, brand owners, contract makers/packers, importers and sellers where their duties overlap.  
**Purpose:** Product discovery and compliance workflow research for the NZ Alcohol module. This is not legal advice, a compliance certification, or a substitute for a winery’s verifier, Customs classification, market importer, or legal adviser.

## Executive summary

Wine compliance is not one checklist. A New Zealand winery commonly operates several overlapping systems:

1. A **Wine Standards Management Plan (WSMP)** under the Wine Act for making wine, covering risk controls, traceability, records and recall.
2. **Customs and excise controls** where alcohol is manufactured or held duty-unpaid in a Customs-controlled area (CCA), with site boundaries, stock movement accounting, entries, payments and nil returns.
3. **Product and label rules** under the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code, Wine Act/Regulations, and—where applicable—destination-market OMARs and geographical indication rules.
4. **Export eligibility and official assurance** administered by MPI, usually batch and consignment specific for New Zealand grape wine, plus Customs export clearance and market-specific evidence.
5. **Sale and Supply of Alcohol Act** licensing, manager, service, remote-sales and delivery requirements for cellar doors, tasting rooms, events and online sales.
6. **Separate food-safety duties** for food prepared or sold alongside wine. A winery WSMP does not automatically cover a café, kitchen, packaged-food activity, or catering operation.
7. Optional or customer-driven systems such as **Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand (SWNZ)**, organic assurance, retailer questionnaires, customer specifications and accounting/inventory workflows.

The operational burden is the joins between these systems. One physical event can need a cellar record, a WSMP trace record, a CCA movement, an export eligibility update, an invoice, a market certificate, and a sustainability record. Small and mid-sized producers often have separate winery software, spreadsheets, MPI Trade Certification, Xero, laboratory portals, verifier forms, freight forwarders, and Customs/TSW workflows. Compliance products can help, but a platform that replaces winemaking judgement or promises “MPI approval” would be unsafe and hard to trust.

The best product opportunity is a **compliance spine attached to the production and sales source of truth**: vineyard block → grape delivery → wine lot/tank → additions, moves and blends → bottling run and label version → location and CCA status → sale/consignee/export consignment. That graph should generate an audit-ready evidence pack, recall/mass-balance report, label/export pre-flight and duty-period reconciliation from records the winery already captures. Do not claim “10× better” without observed winery pilots. Define it as measured reductions in duplicate entry, time to trace, time to prepare an audit/export, and avoidable corrections.

## 1. How to read this report

### Requirement categories

| Tag | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Law / regulator** | Statute, regulation, notice, official rule or licence condition. Exact application depends on the business activity and facts. |
| **Market access** | A destination-market rule or official assurance requirement; market-specific and changeable. |
| **Industry / customer** | Voluntary scheme, retailer/importer specification, or commercial practice. It may be commercially essential without being a general statutory duty. |
| **Product recommendation** | Proposed software support, not a legal requirement. |

The report is current to 8 November 2026. Rules, Customs rates, MPI notices, fees, destination-market requirements, local alcohol policies and vendor offerings can change. The product should store effective dates and source links, and make the operator verify the applied rule when filing or releasing product.

### Important distinctions

- A **WSMP** is not the same thing as a Food Act Food Control Plan (FCP) or National Programme (NP). They can coexist at one winery.
- A **WSMP exemption** does not mean “no Wine Act obligations.” It is a limited exemption from registering/operating under a WSMP in the stated circumstances.
- An **MPI export eligibility statement** is not the same thing as a Customs export entry, an MPI official assurance, a commercial invoice, or the importer’s local clearance.
- A **wine export eligibility status** applies to a defined product/batch and eligible volume, not an unlimited product name or SKU.
- The Wine Regulations’ **85% claim threshold** for New Zealand grape wine is separate from registered **geographical indications (GIs)** and separate again from destination-market composition rules.
- **SWNZ** and organic certification create significant business obligations for participants, but are not universal statutory wine-safety requirements.
- Industry vendor pages show advertised features, not independently verified completeness, usability, or regulatory correctness.

## 2. Business models and who does what

“Winery” is not one operating model. Product discovery should distinguish at least these actors:

| Actor | Typical work and control boundary |
| --- | --- |
| Vineyard grower | Vineyard/block identity, variety, area, spray and other input records, harvest timing, grape quantity/quality, supplier declaration and delivery. Often sells grapes to another wine business. |
| Estate winery | Grows and receives grapes; makes, stores, bottles, labels and sells wine. May run cellar door and export. Usually has the widest WSMP, CCA, licence and recall surface. |
| Contract winemaker | Makes or processes wine for brand owners; records must link client materials, sites, plans, batches and quantities. Clarify who owns WSMP duties, product release, export eligibility, stock and records in the contract. |
| Contract bottler / storage provider | Holds or processes wine at a different site. The plan and CCA arrangements must cover the actual activity and site; brand owner needs evidence and quantity reconciliation. MPI’s WSMP model explicitly asks how contractors/processors are covered. |
| Brand owner / négociant | May buy bulk wine, blend through a contractor, own labels and export or sell without operating a vineyard or winery. Needs control over composition, source records, label claims, packer evidence, eligible volume and customer-facing product. |
| Bulk wine seller/exporter | Manages bulk tank identity, volume, source/composition, analytical/specification data, eligibility, transport/transfer, market document and customer. Bulk wine still has trace and export constraints. |
| Importer / distributor | NZ food-importer registration and imported product/label controls may apply; also responsible for storage, local sale, traceability and recall duties appropriate to its activity. |
| Cellar-door or online seller | Alcohol licence, conditions, age/service/delivery, staff/manager, remote sale and—if food is prepared/sold—separate food-safety activity. |

**Product implication:** permissions and evidence should be scoped by organisation, site, client, role and batch. “The person who typed it” is not necessarily the legal operator or the person responsible for release.

## 3. Core New Zealand wine framework

### 3.1 Wine Act and regulations

The core framework is the [Wine Act 2003](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2003/0114/latest/whole.html), [Wine Regulations 2021](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/regulation/public/2021/0401/latest/whole.html), and MPI notices including the [Wine Notice: Good Operating Practices](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/50368/direct) and [Wine Notice: Export Requirements and Exemptions](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/50374/direct). MPI maintains a [current wine legislation and notices index](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/winemaking-standards-requirements-and-testing/wine-legislation-and-notices).

The law covers wine standards, WSMP registration and verification, export eligibility, exporter registration for some wine types, tracing and recall, records and reporting, recognised agencies/persons, enforcement and cost recovery. Wine businesses should maintain an activity and product scope that matches what they actually make, handle, pack, store and export. A material change in products, process, site, manager, verifier or plan scope can trigger notification, variation or registration requirements.

### 3.2 WSMP registration and operation

Most people who make wine for sale need a registered WSMP. MPI provides an integrated model and the NZ Winegrowers Code of Practice; a custom WSMP must be evaluated. Registration involves the operator, site/activity, selected plan and verifier arrangements. The verifier confirms the plan and MPI registration process; businesses should use the current [MPI registration instructions](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/winemaking-standards-requirements-and-testing/wine-standards-management-plans/registering-a-wine-standards-management-plan).

The plan is risk-based and operational: it needs to manage hazards and contamination so wine is safe and fit for purpose. The [MPI integrated WSMP model](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/52927/direct) describes responsibilities, records, verification and notifications. The system should cover the real premises, equipment, services, people, inputs, processes, storage, packaging and contractors—not just the cellar’s name on a certificate.

The WSMP model expects records that are accurate, readable, identify what happened, when, and who did it, and be kept for at least seven years. It states records must be retrievable within two working days, or within 24 hours if they relate to traceability. Changes that matter to the plan, WSMP manager, verifier, risk management, business activities or cessation have notification deadlines. The exact event/deadline should be checked against the current Act, Regulations, notices and operator’s plan rather than coded as one generic “notify MPI” task.

#### Small winemaker exemption

MPI describes an exemption from the WSMP requirement for a winemaker who makes **less than 20,000 litres over a 2-year period**, notifies MPI, and does not sell wine for export. It lasts two years and does not waive the underlying Wine Act standards, record/trace obligations, or other laws. The cellar-door situation has a narrow customer-arranged export condition: the winemaker cannot export on that customer’s behalf. See [MPI’s exemption page](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/winemaking-standards-requirements-and-testing/wine-businesses-exempt-from-registering-a-wine-standards-management-plan). There are distinct exemptions for businesses that only label bottled wine or store bottled wine before a later winemaking process; operators should test the exact scope with MPI.

**Product implication:** support the exemption as an explicit, evidence-backed operating status with threshold tracking and an expiry/review date; do not silently treat an exempt small maker as “not regulated.”

### 3.3 Recent 2026 changes to keep effective-dated

The [Wine Amendment Regulations (No 2) 2026](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/secondary-legislation/pco-drafted/2026/215/en/latest/) commenced 28 August 2026. The amendments introduce a minor-WSMP-change process and amend water, personnel contamination, wine-business traceability, exporter traceability and recall provisions. For software, treat the revised provisions as effective from 28 August 2026 and preserve the version that applied to earlier records. The exact duties should be configured from the operative text, not inferred from a prior template.

The [Wine Amendment Regulations 2026](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/secondary-legislation/pco-drafted/2026/156/en/latest/) commenced 1 July 2026. They changed wine export certification cost recovery: the default wine export levy is **$0.0037/L**, with a threshold described by MPI as payable on each litre exported after the first **200,000 L** per exporter; MPI Trade Certification document and processing fees now apply. The regulations state fees including $7.33 for an export eligibility certificate and an additional $21.64 in specified processing cases. See the [MPI fees page](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/winemaking-standards-requirements-and-testing/wine-industry-fees-and-charges) and the amendment text. Store fee/rate schedules with date, product and charging basis; never bake an old export levy into a static calculator.

The Sale and Supply of Alcohol (Improving Alcohol Regulation) Amendment Bill remains a **bill**, not current law, as at this report date: it is at the select-committee stage in the [Parliamentary bill history](https://bills.parliament.nz/v/6/b98f4d35-d0bd-47ab-a326-08de82fd2ebd?Tab=history&lang=en). Monitor it, but do not encode proposals as current licensee duties.

## 4. Food safety and winery operating controls

### 4.1 Wine process hazards and good operating practice

Wine businesses need documented controls that fit their actual process and products. The MPI Good Operating Practice notice and WSMP model cover, among other things:

- premises, winery layout, cleanable surfaces, equipment and process separation;
- water suitable for its use, including source, treatment, testing and corrective action where relevant;
- cleaning and sanitation, maintenance, maintenance compounds and chemical storage;
- pest management, waste, drains and environmental contamination pathways;
- staff illness, personal hygiene, training, protective clothing and contamination controls;
- receiving grapes, water and winemaking inputs; supplier approval and suitability;
- processing, fermentation, movement, storage, packaging and transport controls;
- product specifications, sampling/testing, non-conforming product and release decisions;
- complaints, corrective actions, contamination response, traceability and recall;
- contractor, mobile/cross-site and offsite work coverage.

For grape suppliers, the winemaker should obtain and retain relevant information about spray types, withholding periods and last applications, other vineyard inputs and contamination concerns. At intake, the winery needs a defensible record of supplier, date, quantity, identity/variety/vintage/area and condition; record any acceptance, rejection, segregation or corrective action. The precise record set depends on product/process, source and the applicable notice and WSMP.

This is not NP3 for wine. The winery WSMP is the sector-specific risk management plan. Existing generic food safety controls can support a shared implementation, but a spirits NP3 workflow cannot simply be copied and labelled as a wine plan.

### 4.2 Separate Food Act activities

Food service around a winery needs a separate activity assessment under the [Food Act 2014](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2014/0032/latest/whole.html), Food Regulations 2015 and Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code. A WSMP does not automatically cover a cellar-door café or kitchen. A business may need an FCP or National Programme, or a defined exemption depending on exactly what food it handles. MPI offers a [Food Safety Template for Winemakers](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/running-a-food-business/food-control-plans/use-a-template-food-control-plan) and a [cellar-door food guide](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64740/direct). Its [food-business registration guide](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/starting-a-food-business/register-food-business) should be used for the actual activity mix.

Examples:

- A cellar door offering only certain sealed, shelf-stable packaged foods may have a different registration status from a business that prepares food. Do not generalise this into an automatic exemption.
- Preparing meals, reheating/cooking, catering, handling unpackaged foods or selling from a kitchen can trigger an FCP/registration requirement depending on activity.
- Alcohol tastings do not remove food duties. Food service and statutory tasting-snack conditions need to be considered together.
- Food businesses and exporters can have their own recall notification duties; the responsible entity and clock may differ from wine-specific notification.

**Product implication:** a guided activity-scope questionnaire should identify wine-making vs food service vs import/export vs retail, then point to registrations and separate plans. It should not represent a generic checklist as legal classification.

### 4.3 Allergens, additives and wine composition

Wine inputs and processing aids can affect label statements and product release. Added sulphites at **10 mg/kg or more** must be declared under the Food Standards Code. Other allergen declarations may be relevant if egg, milk/casein, fish-derived fining agents or other allergenic substances are present in the final food at the applicable threshold/condition. Keep a formulation and fining/processing-aid record tied to each wine lot and label version. Avoid assuming a processing aid never matters; apply the Code and current MPI/FSANZ guidance to the actual use and residual presence.

Alcoholic beverages above the applicable strength threshold generally have exemptions from ingredient-list and nutrition-information-panel requirements, but claims and special mandatory declarations have separate rules. This does not remove the duty to ensure wine is safe or to maintain composition/process records.

## 5. Traceability, records, recall and evidence

### 5.1 Required source-to-recipient chain

Wine Regulations 77–80 and the Wine Act trace/recall provisions require wine businesses to keep an adequate traceability procedure. At minimum, a useful system needs to answer “what went into this wine?”, “where did this wine go?”, “what else is affected?” and “can the volumes reconcile?”

The provenance graph should cover:

1. **Vineyard/source:** grower, block/area, variety, vintage, GI/region, spray and withholding data, harvest date and quantity.
2. **Receipt/intake:** delivery docket/weigh ticket, supplier, site, date/time, accepted/rejected volume, condition, sampling and any exception.
3. **Wine making:** crush/press, fermentation vessel, transfers/racking, blending, additions, processing aids, yields/losses, retentions and relevant analytical results.
4. **Packaging:** bottling or other package run, line, date, quantity, packaging-material lots, label artwork/version, product code and finished-goods lot.
5. **Movement/storage:** inter-site and contractor movements, tank/bulk transfer, CCA boundary events, warehouse and location, status/duty class.
6. **Distribution:** customer/consignee, invoice/order, quantity, date, market, shipment, export eligibility/assurance references and returns/withdrawals.

Traceability is about suppliers and recipients and enough information to carry out a recall. It is not satisfied by a batch name alone, a PDF folder with no link to affected stock, or a wine inventory total without its inputs and destinations. A mass-balance view is a strong operational control: source/volume consumed should reconcile to current tank/package stock, loss, samples, transfers, sales and disposal.

### 5.2 Records to retain and produce

The MPI integrated WSMP model says retain required records for at least seven years and make them accessible. Traceability records requested by a Wine Officer/DG have a 24-hour or shorter response period under the current traceability provisions, while the model also describes the wider 2-working-day retrieval standard for other records. Set records up for exportable, rapid retrieval and keep the source artefact plus structured fields.

Practical record inventory:

| Record family | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Governance | Registered WSMP, plan version/scope, site map, operator, day-to-day manager, verifier, verification reports, corrective actions, notifications and change log. |
| Staff and hygiene | Training/competence, role authorisations, illness/contamination controls, cleaning schedules and signoffs. |
| Equipment/site | Maintenance, calibration, cleaning, sanitation, water tests/treatment, pest control, chemicals/maintenance compounds, waste and environmental controls. |
| Suppliers and grapes | Supplier details, grower declarations, vineyard/block, variety, vintage, area/GI, spray and withholding details, weigh tickets, supplier plan where relevant and acceptance notes. |
| Inputs | Supplier, identity, lot, date, amount, certificate/specification where relevant, storage, use in wine batch, processing aid/allergen implications. |
| Batch/work | Unique wine/batch identifiers, operations, dates, people, vessels, quantities, temperatures or process controls as applicable, additions, transfers, blend composition, losses, test results and product disposition. |
| Contamination/non-conformance | Detection, affected wine/input, quantity/lot, risk decision, containment, notification, corrective action and final disposition. |
| Packaging/labels | Bottling/packing run, packaging materials, product/label version, proof/review, quantity, rejects/rework and finished lot. |
| Export | Market requirements/OMAR version, eligibility per batch and volume, samples/test results, EES, official assurance, export levy/fees, importer/consignee, shipment and Customs clearance records. |
| Sales/distribution | Order/invoice, customer/consignee, stock lot allocation, date/quantity, returns, credits and movement/disposal. |
| Recall | Decision log, reason, health/risk assessment, affected batches/volumes, supplier/customer contacts, recovered/remaining stock, MPI notices and effectiveness exercise. |
| CCA/excise | CCA licence/site map and boundary, product classifications, daily inventory, receipts, manufacture, inter-CCA movements, removals, exports, loss/destruction, entries, payment and nil-return evidence. |
| Commercial/industry schemes | SWNZ records, organic certificates/OMPs, retailer specs, customer audits, water/consent, insurance and customer-specific declarations where applicable. |

### 5.3 Recall and annual simulation

WSMP operators and wine exporters must maintain recall procedures and conduct a simulated recall at least every 12 months under the wine rules. The exact recall notification route and time should be checked for the operator’s WSMP and current rules; MPI general guidance says certain regulated businesses must notify New Zealand Food Safety as soon as practicable and no later than 24 hours after deciding to recall. A wine business also has a 24-hour notification obligation for critical/export non-compliance under MPI’s WSMP materials.

A useful drill should be able to start from either an input or a finished lot, find affected outputs and customers, account for volumes, distinguish sold/held/returned/exported stock, time the task, record gaps and assign corrective actions. The result must be a drill report; test data must not trigger a real customer recall or contaminate a real lot.

## 6. Composition, labels, claims and GI controls

### 6.1 Domestic label requirements

Use the current [MPI alcoholic-drinks label guidance](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/labelling-composition-food-drinks/specific-product-labelling/labelling-requirements-alcoholic-drinks-including-wine) and FSANZ code as the source of truth. A packaged alcohol product may need:

- product identity and country-of-origin information;
- alcohol content statement in the required format for the product strength;
- standard drinks statement for beverages at or above 0.5% ABV;
- pregnancy warning label for packaged alcoholic beverages above 1.15% ABV for retail sale;
- allergen declarations, including added sulphites at/above the required threshold;
- lot/batch identification and business identity/contact details under relevant food/labelling rules;
- mandatory nutrition/energy statement where applicable after the transition; and
- claims/content restrictions and any market-specific language.

The standard-drink calculation in NZ uses 10g ethanol: `package volume (mL) × ABV fraction × 0.789 g/mL ÷ 10 g`. For a 750mL bottle at 12.5% ABV, this is approximately 7.4 standard drinks, displayed according to rounding/label rules. Alcohol energy declarations have a three-year transition ending **13 August 2028** following the 2025 change; use the current [FSANZ alcohol-energy calculator](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/labelling/alcohol-energy-content-calculator/online-calculator) and track the package’s applicable transition/version. Do not treat a calculator result as substitute for artwork review.

### 6.2 Wine-specific origin/variety/vintage claims

For New Zealand grape wine, a variety, vintage or geographical-area claim generally needs at least **85%** of the wine to meet that claim under the Wine Regulations; combinations and blend descriptions have specific rules. Some rules differ for imported wine, bulk wine, or destination markets, and foreign-market specifications can be stricter. Keep blend proportions as immutable, versioned inputs to a label decision; a later top-up or blend change should invalidate prior claim checks.

Registered GI is a separate legal layer under the [Geographical Indications (Wine and Spirits) Registration Act 2006](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2006/0060/latest/whole.html). Check the [IPONZ register](https://www.iponz.govt.nz/get-ip/geographical-indications/register/?location=nz) and source/GI definitions for the exact claim. Export-market treaties and the importing country can protect or restrict terms. “85% threshold passed” does not mean a term is a registered GI or the label is valid in a foreign market.

### 6.3 Label workflow opportunity

Build a label pre-flight from a specific product composition and package:

1. identify wine category, origin, imported/locally made status, ABV, package size, ingredients/processing aids and intended claims;
2. calculate standard drinks, relevant energy declaration, sulphite/allergen statements and origin/variety/vintage/GI eligibility;
3. add destination-market requirements and the effective date/rule source;
4. store the artwork, proofing decisions, who approved it, and its product/lot applicability;
5. invalidate the approval when ABV, composition, supplier/source, package or claim changes.

The product should show “review needed” for unresolved or non-machine-verifiable claims instead of inventing a pass.

## 7. Customs, excise and CCA operations

### 7.1 CCA applicability and physical control

New Zealand manufactured alcohol is excisable. A Customs-controlled area licence applies where excisable alcohol is manufactured, stored duty-unpaid or otherwise handled within the licensed arrangement. Customs lists Licensed Manufacturing Areas (including fermentation, blending, ageing and bottling), Off-Site Storage (duty-unpaid storage away from manufacturing) and Export Warehouse types. See [Customs CCA licences](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/excise/excise-licensing/excise-customs-controlled-area-licences) and the [CCA overview](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/customs-controlled-areas).

The winery has to agree its actual site layout, licensed boundaries, activity and movement controls with Customs. Do not assume the WSMP site or warehouse boundary equals the CCA boundary. A bottle leaving the CCA is generally a removal for home consumption unless a specified authorised movement, temporary movement, export, or movement to export warehouse exception applies. This is a physical movement/duty-status rule, not a point-of-sale rule.

### 7.2 Excise classification, rate and filing

Find the excise item in Customs’ [Excise and Excise-equivalent Duties Table](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/excise/excise-declarants/excise-items-and-duty-rates). Wine classification depends on product category, strength, ingredients and item wording; fortified wine and unusual fermented products may not use the same item or rate basis as ordinary grape table wine. Duty may be calculated per litre of beverage or per litre of alcohol. If classification is uncertain, request a Customs ruling/advice rather than guess.

Rates are indexed and can change each 1 July. The official [2026 rates notice](https://www.customs.govt.nz/about-us/news/important-notices/new-excise-duty-rates-for-alcohol-from-1-july-2026) is the current rate reference as at this report date. Store classification/rate source, effective dates, unit, strength, volume, duty status and approved calculation for each product. Separate excise duty, alcohol levy, GST and MPI wine-export cost recovery.

Newly licensed businesses generally lodge and pay monthly. Customs can approve 6- or 12-month cycles if the licensee demonstrates consistent compliance and suitable systems; a low-volume or seasonal producer may seek an appropriate cycle. Alcohol entries and payments are normally due by the last working day of the month after removal. A nil return is still required for a period with no removal when the licence remains active and relevant goods are held. See [entry timing](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/excise/entry-lodgement-timing), [paying excise](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/excise/excise-duty/pay-excise-duty-and-other-charges), and [excise entries/nil returns](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/excise/excise-declarants/excise-entries).

### 7.3 CCA record and movement ledger

Customs expects records that support accurate accounting for goods in the licensed area, their manufacture, receipt, storage, movement, removal, export, loss/destruction and duty paid. Confirm current record form and retention/storage rules against the current [CCA alcohol record-keeping guidance](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/excise/alcohol-and-excise/record-keeping-obligations-for-alcohol-licenced-manufacturing-areas-and-off-site-storage) and Customs’ amended regulations notice (effective 10 September 2026). Records need to reconcile physical stock and declaration quantities.

The software should model **events**, not just a monthly sales total:

- produced/received into the licensed area;
- transfers between licensed premises/locations;
- samples, tastings, process use, spoilage and loss;
- destruction or other disposal, with approval/evidence where required;
- sale/delivery inside the licensed area if allowed by licence arrangement;
- physical removal for home consumption;
- duty-suspended transfer to another CCA;
- removal for export/export warehouse;
- corrections, adjustments, recounts and period close.

Each event needs quantity, strength, package or bulk unit, source lot, destination, timestamp, operator, authorisation/evidence and whether it changes duty status. Reconcile the opening balance + production/receipts − movements/removals/losses = closing balance by item/site/duty status.

### 7.4 Export clearance is a second system

MPI export eligibility/assurance does not clear goods through the border. Commercial exporters also need Customs export clearance through Trade Single Window, correct export entry or Cargo Report Export according to the shipment/consignment circumstances, invoice/packing/transport documents, and importer-side requirements. See [Customs clear your exports](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/export/clear-your-exports). The product can build a pre-flight and evidence bundle, but should not represent an MPI EES as a Customs export declaration.

## 8. Exporting wine from New Zealand

### 8.1 New Zealand grape wine

MPI’s [export steps](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/export/food/exporting-new-zealand-grape-wine/steps-to-exporting-new-zealand-grape-wine) require New Zealand grape wine to meet Wine Act export eligibility and applicable destination-market access conditions. In outline:

1. The making/packing activities are covered by a WSMP or applicable permitted arrangement and are verified.
2. The exporter identifies a homogeneous, stable, finished batch and keeps its composition/source/process records.
3. Eligibility is applied for through MPI Trade Certification. Required analysis/sample/market-specific tests and labels are attached as relevant.
4. The eligible product/batch and quantity are linked to export documents and actual consignment.
5. An Export Eligibility Statement (EES) and any required official assurance are obtained before shipment; quantity exported must not exceed eligible volume.
6. Customs export entry/clearance, freight and the importer’s destination requirements are completed separately.

MPI’s export notice sets eligibility time windows, with packaged wine generally eligible up to two years and bulk wine generally up to three months under the notice, subject to the exact product and market conditions. An experienced winemaker assessment, sample retention, product readiness/stability and limits on post-sample adjustments apply. Treat these as versioned export-notice rules and verify the precise clause before operational use.

Exemptions are narrow:

- Trade samples up to **110 L** in one consignment can be exempt from export-eligibility requirements if not for retail sale and bottles are clearly “not for sale”; the destination market may still require documents.
- A trade consignment up to **27 L** may be exempt from an EES if the wine already has current eligibility in MPI Trade Certification and specified exporter/consignee/volume/destination records are kept. OMARs may override or vary this.
- Aircraft-consumption and other specific cases have their own terms.

Do not turn an exemption into a blanket “small export” bypass. The [MPI notice](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/50374/direct) and current OMAR take precedence.

### 8.2 Fruit/vegetable wine, cider, mead, wine products and non-NZ grape wine

The regime differs by product. MPI provides a separate [export path for fruit/vegetable wine, cider, mead, wine products and non-NZ grape wine](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/export/food/exporting-fruit-or-vegetable-wine-cider-mead-non-nz-grape-wine-and-wine-products/steps-to-exporting-fruit-or-vegetable-wine-cider-mead-non-nz-grape-wine-and-wine-products). Exporter registration may be required for product types other than NZ grape wine; a business can use a registered exporter where appropriate. Food Act requirements, product category, market and organic status change the route. Make the user select and substantiate the product’s legal category rather than choosing “wine” as a generic export type.

### 8.3 OMARs, export documents and market rules

An Overseas Market Access Requirement (OMAR) may add conditions to the New Zealand baseline: approved premises, process, analytical results, labelling, blend/origin rules, certificates, import permits or official assurance. MPI publishes market documents for markets such as the EU, Great Britain, United States and Brazil. Other countries may have no NZ OMAR, but local law still applies and the importer should confirm requirements.

Common document and evidence types include:

- MPI export eligibility record/EES and official assurance;
- market-specific VI-1 or certificate of analysis/health/free-sale/origin forms where prescribed;
- batch analytical report and sample/eligibility record;
- label and composition/specification;
- commercial invoice, packing list, shipping/transport and Customs declaration;
- buyer/importer details, destination, volumes and export/levy fee accounting;
- organic official assurance/certificate if the wine is marketed as organic.

MPI’s [requirements and documents page](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/export/food/exporting-new-zealand-grape-wine/requirement-documents-for-exporting-wine) is the best starting point. Requirement scope changes by market and product category. The product should store the version/date of the OMAR and show the exact source at time of release.

### 8.4 Export non-compliance and organic claims

MPI requires prompt reporting of critical and export non-compliance; its [CNC/ENC guidance](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/export/food/exporting-new-zealand-grape-wine/critical-and-export-non-compliance-of-wine) explains the routes. WSMP model instructions say notify MPI within 24 hours upon awareness of CNC/ENC. Provide a hold/stop-ship workflow and decision log; it must not let a user quietly relabel affected wine as eligible.

Organic export assurance is an additional programme, not a label toggle. Under MPI’s [Official Organic Assurance Programme](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/export/food/organics/export-under-the-official-organic-assurance-programme), covered exports need the required organic management/certification and official assurance for destination markets. Organic inputs, chain of custody, conversion status, separation, processing, packaging and claims must be recorded. The wine export and organic paths have to agree on the same batch and consignment quantities.

## 9. Domestic selling, cellar door, tasting and delivery

### 9.1 Licensing and operating constraints

The [Sale and Supply of Alcohol Act 2012](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2012/0120/latest/whole.html) regulates sale and supply. A winery/cellar door may require an off-licence and/or on-licence depending on what is sold, where it is consumed, and whether customers are served samples. Licence conditions, manager certificates, opening hours, local alcohol policy, food requirements, host responsibility and licence renewal are local and site-specific. Health New Zealand’s [winery and cellar-door licensing factsheet](https://resources.alcohol.org.nz/assets/management-and-laws/Alcohol-Licensing-Infosheets-2025/4.1-AL1131-B-Wineries-and-Cellar-Doors.pdf) is a practical overview.

The 2024 Winery Cellar Door Tasting Amendment Act changed the rules for qualifying winery cellar-door tastings; the precise licence and snack-food conditions need to be checked in the [amending Act](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2024/29/en/latest/) and current licence. Do not assume all tastings, events or producers qualify.

Online and remote sales still need the appropriate off-licence and its conditions. The seller should display required licence information, use age-verification controls at sale and delivery, follow permitted delivery hours/conditions and ensure staff/contractors understand their obligations. Remote order acceptance, dispatch, proof of age, failed delivery, returns and refunds should be a joined workflow. The 2026 reform bill is not yet law at the research date.

### 9.2 Cellar door as a cross-module workflow

One cellar-door session can touch:

- tasting room licence and duty manager coverage;
- samples poured/stock used and CCA status;
- staff/volunteer training and host responsibility;
- snack/food offer and food-plan controls;
- event booking, capacity and local condition;
- age check and sale transaction;
- inventory reduction, invoice/receipt and excise movement;
- complaint, incident or refusal-of-service record.

Do not automatically calculate excise on POS sales. Reconcile the product’s physical movement and CCA/duty status as directed by Customs and the licence arrangement. Product-specific legal review is needed for sampling and tasting classification.

## 10. Voluntary, commercial and adjacent obligations

### 10.1 Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand

SWNZ is an industry sustainability programme, **not a substitute for a WSMP and not a general statutory prerequisite**. It can be commercially important because wineries, distributors and export buyers may expect certification. The current [SWNZ standard (May 2026)](https://www.nzwine.com/media/xkvl1gjb/swnz-standard-version-1-may-2026.pdf) and [programme page](https://www.nzwine.com/en/sustainability/swnz/) cover vineyard/winery sustainability areas such as spray application records, water use, wastewater, resource consent, waste, energy, biodiversity, people and governance. Participants should check the current standard, audit cycle and member requirements. SWNZ may require annual reporting and periodic audits; distinguish membership, certification and each site’s status.

### 10.2 NZ Winegrowers levy and export cost recovery

MPI says wine businesses that sell grapes or wine may have a New Zealand Winegrowers statutory levy obligation under the applicable commodity levy order. Separately, the Wine Regulations now recover MPI Trade Certification costs through user fees and a revised export levy. These have distinct liable parties, bases, thresholds and filing processes. The product should avoid rolling them into a single vague “wine levy” and show the source/date/liable entity.

### 10.3 Organic, water, wastewater, biosecurity and worker safety

Depending on site and claims, wineries may also need to manage resource consents and discharge/water take conditions, workplace health and safety, hazardous substances, biosecurity risks, packaging and waste obligations, privacy, consumer law, employment and tax/accounting. These are material adjacent controls but outside the narrow wine module’s complete legal coverage. Surface them as integrations or clearly scoped referral/checklist items after validation with the relevant agencies and councils.

## 11. Seasonal operating cycle and tasks

The user journey needs to match the winery calendar and not only an inspector’s checklist.

### Pre-vintage

- confirm WSMP scope, verifier, staff/training, contractor/third-party plans and equipment readiness;
- inspect/maintain tanks, presses, lines, calibration and water systems;
- approve growers/suppliers, get spray/withholding declarations and harvest forecasts;
- configure harvest intake, batch and vessel IDs, labels/products and stock locations;
- check CCA boundary, licensed activity, inter-site routes and duty-period calendar;
- confirm laboratory arrangements and destination-market/export plans.

### Vintage and winemaking

- receive/weigh grape loads; confirm source, variety, vintage, supplier and spray records;
- sample/assess fruit and document accept/reject/segregate/corrective decisions;
- crush/press/ferment and record actual quantities, identifiers, vessels, additions and operations;
- track transfers, blend proportions, losses, samples, lab results and deviations;
- manage contamination, hold/release and rework decisions with reason and approver;
- log CCA receipts, production and transfers in the site’s correct duty status.

### Cellar, release and bottling

- reconcile tank and bulk inventory; verify blend/claim constraints and label version;
- review analytical results and product release status;
- plan contract bottling and confirm site/WSMP/CCA coverage;
- capture run, package material lots, yields, rejects, label proof, lot code and finished volume;
- confirm export eligibility status and market-specific preflight before booking shipment;
- reconcile completed quantity to sold, held, sample, loss and closing stock.

### Selling and shipment

- quote/order/allocate stock to invoice, distributor or export consignment;
- reserve and decrement eligible export volume only for the right batch and market;
- prepare EES/official assurance and separate Customs clearance documents;
- record customer/consignee, destination, shipment, sale, stock movement, duty treatment and export fees;
- manage online/remote sales age checks, dispatch, failed delivery, return or credit;
- complete monthly excise entry/payment or approved 6-/12-month period and nil return when required.

### Periodic and event-driven

- annual WSMP verification (intervals and exceptions depend on risk/performance and current rules);
- simulated recall at least annually;
- update plan/registration for significant changes and submit minor-change notifications on current schedule;
- reconcile CCA physical stock, records and declarations;
- renew licences and manager certificates; update local conditions;
- refresh label, energy, tariff, fee and OMAR rules by effective date;
- manage complaint, incident, contamination, CNC/ENC, hold, recall and product disposal;
- renew SWNZ/organic/retailer certification and evidence when applicable.

### What the market proves—and does not prove

New Zealand wine is export-dependent and geographically/compositionally diverse. NZ Wine’s [2026 annual report](https://www.nzwine.com/en/media/nzw-annual-report/) reports approximately NZ$2.108b export value and 306.2m litres to June 2026; its [2026 vineyard report](https://www.nzwine.com/en/media/statistics-reports/vineyard-reports/) reports 44,229 planted hectares, 2,019 active vineyards and a mean vineyard size of 22ha, with Marlborough about 73% of area. These figures indicate real destination and provenance complexity. They do not establish software willingness to pay or any vendor’s market share.

Likely friction to validate with operators:

- duplicate data entry among cellar software, spreadsheet, Xero, lab, MPI and Customs;
- the same lot described differently by grower, winemaker, packer, exporter and customer;
- manual blend/origin calculations and label approvals after changes;
- reconciling planned, actual and stored volumes across tank, packaging, sample/loss, warehouse and CCA;
- small teams carrying compliance knowledge in one person’s head;
- seasonal pressure and intermittent connectivity at vineyard/cellar;
- proving a third-party contractor’s plan/site coverage and returning complete source records;
- market-by-market export requirements and eligibility volume tracking;
- keeping annual or event-driven deadlines current as notices, fees, tariffs and standards change;
- the perception that compliance software adds another form instead of removing existing work.

These are hypotheses from workflow structure and public market materials, not findings from interviews. Customer discovery must measure their frequency, cost and severity.

## 14. How to be meaningfully better (and test the “10×” claim)

### Product thesis

Do not compete on the number of checkboxes. Compete on **one operational fact captured once and reused with proof**. A winery should be able to run its business, then answer an auditor, customer, Customs officer or importer from the same traceable event chain, with the right boundary and rule version.

### Defensible “order of magnitude” targets

These are test targets, not performance claims:

1. **Recall:** trace a supplier input to all tanks, packages, customers and export consignments in under 10 minutes, with a reconciled volume and no spreadsheet join.
2. **Evidence:** generate a current WSMP evidence packet and missing-record list in minutes, with each field linked to a source event/document and named owner.
3. **Export preflight:** detect missing/expired batch eligibility, market evidence, label version, certificate and volume before booking/dispatch; eliminate preventable last-minute rework.
4. **Label change impact:** when a blend, ABV, allergen or source changes, identify every affected claim, artwork and stock lot rather than rechecking manually from scratch.
5. **Excise close:** reconcile CCA opening/production/transfer/removal/loss/closing quantities from movement events and distinguish a zero-removal nil return from an unlicensed/inactive period.
6. **No duplicate entry:** reuse supplier, production, stock, customer and shipment records across WSMP, recall, export, CCA, sales and accounting exports; measure repeated fields and corrections.
7. **Seasonal usability:** capture a grape load or cellar operation on a phone with poor connectivity, visibly queue/sync it, prevent duplicates on retry and expose conflicts rather than silently overwriting.
8. **Cross-company handoff:** share only the batch/client records needed with a grower, contract maker, bottler, verifier, forwarder or importer and preserve a signed/audited hand-off.

Measure each against the winery’s actual present workflow. “10×” should mean a measured ratio or a dramatic quality improvement on a named task, not marketing language.

### Trust requirements

- Every rule/check links to primary source, jurisdiction, product scope, effective date and last reviewed date.
- Label law, WSMP, market requirements, Customs, licensing, SWNZ and organic statuses stay distinct.
- Human approval is explicit for product release, blend claims, eligibility, export, excise declaration, hold release and recall closure.
- Store evidence and calculated result snapshots so a later rule update does not rewrite history.
- Never claim “MPI-approved,” “Customs compliant,” or “automatically compliant” without formal authority; describe what the software checked and what remains an operator decision.
- Tenant isolation includes every child record, uploaded file, supplier/client portal, export packet and background job; contract producers are a heightened cross-tenant risk.
- No secrets or unrelated customers’ commercial data in generated audit/export bundles.

## 15. Recommended delivery sequence

### Stage 0: validate the workflow before building wine ERP

Run observed discovery with 5–8 businesses, intentionally covering: an estate winery with cellar door, a small domestic-only exempt maker, an exporter, a contract maker/brand owner, a contract bottler/storage relationship, an organic/SWNZ participant, and a wine seller/importer. Include at least one small team and one multi-site exporter. Have a practicing winemaker and independent verifier review the workflow; use an excise accountant/Customs adviser for CCA duty boundaries; obtain market/importer input for export.

For each, map one real vintage batch from source to sale/export and one recall drill. Record current tools, who does each step, elapsed time, transcription/rework, overlooked exceptions, data exportability, setup burden and what an existing vendor already does well. No competitor dismissal without a same-task live comparison.

### Stage 1: compliance spine

- wine business/site/product registry and regime scope;
- grower, vineyard block, intake and supplier declarations;
- wine lot, tank, transfer, additions, blend and actual yield/loss graph;
- packaging/label version and lot identity;
- rapid source/recipient search, volume reconciliation and annual drill report;
- WSMP evidence, deadlines and verification/plan change register;
- robust CSV/document import/export and migration path.

### Stage 2: label and export assurance workspace

- New Zealand and destination-market label preflight with effective-dated rule sources;
- batch eligibility, sample/lab evidence, EES/official assurance status and eligible-volume balance;
- export packet that separates MPI certification, Customs clearance, importer documents and fees;
- optional organic assurance overlay;
- export levy and per-document cost schedule with annual effective-date change control.

### Stage 3: CCA/excise + sales + partner workflows

- configured CCA site/boundaries, duty status and movement ledger;
- period reconciliation, entry/nil-return workflow and proof of approved filing;
- cellar door and remote-sale licence/condition tasks; samples, food activities and delivery exceptions;
- scoped grower/contractor/bottler/verifier/exporter collaboration;
- Xero, lab and e-commerce integrations where customer demand and APIs permit.

### Later, only if validated

Full vineyard management, advanced cellar production planning, lab instrumentation, tank/barrel costing, forecasting, winery accounting, order management, route planning or replacing a mature vintrace/Vinsight/Cepaos installation. The initial advantage can be an interoperable compliance layer without forcing the customer to abandon specialist cellar tools.

## 16. Open questions and boundaries

This report is comprehensive desk research, but not “everything” in the sense of every winery’s site-specific licence, every destination OMAR, or a legal opinion. Resolve these before building rules as enforcement:

1. Confirm exact current 2026 wording and operator interpretation for revised traceability and minor WSMP changes with MPI/verifiers.
2. Confirm whether the winery is a maker, exempt maker, packer, storage operator, exporter, importer, or several—and map each site/contractor.
3. Validate CCA boundary, product item classification, approved movement/entry cycle and special-use treatment with Customs.
4. Check current Wine Notice clauses for eligibility duration, sample retention, permitted post-sample changes and each export product category.
5. For each target market, obtain current OMAR/importer requirements and separate them from general NZ requirements.
6. Confirm cellar-door licence type and conditions, including the precise 2024 tasting amendment application and local alcohol policy.
7. Classify every food activity separately; assess FCP/NP/registration with MPI or verifier.
8. Validate GI, imported-wine blend and label claim rules for each actual source and market.
9. Confirm current SWNZ and organic participant criteria, audit schedule and evidence requirements with those programmes.
10. Run hands-on competitor comparisons and producer interviews before asserting pain severity, category gaps, or superior value.
