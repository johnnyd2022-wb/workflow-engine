## 1. The scenario

**Problem (chemical hazard).** A lot of dried juniper berries is found to contain berries from a toxic look-alike plant, savin juniper (*Juniperus sabina*). Savin is poisonous and is not a gin botanical. Its oil is volatile, so we cannot assume it is removed when we distil.

**Why this one.** It could really happen here. We buy wild-harvested dried berries from a supplier, the berries go into every gin, and the gin is sold to trade customers and directly to consumers. That makes it a worst case for us: a consumer-level recall involving several batches and several kinds of customer.

**Back-story.**

- Day −98: 20 kg of dried juniper berries (supplier lot `SIM-JB-14`, four 5 kg bags) were received and booked in.
- Day −95 to Day −40: 8 kg went into four gin batches, `SIM-G-101` to `SIM-G-104`. 2 kg went into a recipe trial that was not sold. 10 kg is still in the store.
- Day 0, 08:40: our supplier emails to say another of their customers had the same lot tested and savin berries were found. They suggest a mix-up when the pickers' bags were combined at the collection point. They tell us to stop using the lot.

**Other causes we would have to rule out** (an investigation does not assume the first answer):

| Possible cause | Why it is plausible | How we would check |
|---|---|---|
| Toxic look-alike berries mixed in (this scenario) | Wild harvest, bags combined before export | Lab identification of berries; supplier's harvest and packing records |
| Pesticide or fumigant residue | Dried botanicals are often treated in storage or transit | Ask for the supplier's certificate of analysis; residue test on retained berries |
| Mould toxins (aflatoxin, ochratoxin A) | Damp storage or slow drying | Mycotoxin test; look at the berries and the store |
| Heavy metals | Soil or drying equipment | Metals test on berries and on the finished gin |
| Mix-up or cross-contamination at the supplier's packing | Shared packing line | Supplier's packing records and their other customers |

Mould toxins and metals stay mostly in the still residue, while a volatile oil can come through into the spirit. That is why the lab is asked to test the gin as well as the berries.

## 5. Risk assessment — SIMULATED RECALL

- **Product and who eats or drinks it:** Whistlebird gin, 700 mL, batches SIM-G-101 to 104. Sold to adults, including people who may be pregnant or unwell.
- **Hazard and likely harm:** a toxic look-alike berry (savin juniper) may have been distilled into the gin. Savin is documented as poisonous and as unsafe in pregnancy. The level in the finished gin is unknown, and the volatile oil can carry into a spirit.
- **Source and time window:** supplier lot SIM-JB-14, received Day −98, used between Day −95 and Day −40.
- **Affected lots and quantity:** four batches, 1,500 bottles. 430 on hold at Whistlebird, 815 with eight trade customers, 211 sold to consumers (135 cellar door, 76 online), 22 with staff and events, 16 retained samples, 6 unaccounted for.
- **Where it is now:** section 3 and section 8.
- **What we do not know:** the amount of toxic material in the gin, the destination of the 6 unaccounted bottles, how much of the cellar door gin has been drunk.
- **Advice sought:** NZFS and our verifier (**0800 00 83 33**, **Food.Recalls@mpi.govt.nz**).

## 6. Decision

- **Decision:** consumer-level recall of Whistlebird gin batches SIM-G-101, SIM-G-102, SIM-G-103 and SIM-G-104, and a hold on the remaining berries from lot SIM-JB-14.
- **Why:** the hazard is a poison, it cannot be ruled out of the finished gin without a lab result, and 211 bottles have already gone to consumers. Waiting for results before acting would leave people exposed. If the lab later shows the gin is clear, the recall can be closed and stock released, recorded as a separate decision.
- **Made by:** either Johnny or Nikolai can authorise this. Both take part in the exercise.
- **Held stock:** not released until its safety is established and the release is recorded.
